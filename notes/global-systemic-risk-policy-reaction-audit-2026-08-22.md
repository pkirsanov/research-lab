## Bottom line

The transcript identifies several real stresses, but it repeatedly converts a
gross disruption into an equal downstream production loss. That conversion is
usually wrong. Inventories, rerouting, allocation priority, substitution,
recycling, demand destruction, contractual protection, and policy response
absorb different shares of each shock.

The evidence supports a difficult adjustment with expensive assets, weak labor
growth, commodity inflation, and occasional large corrections. It does not make
a 2008-style financial collapse the base case. Household mortgage leverage,
bank capital, bank liquidity, and loan performance are much stronger than they
were before the global financial crisis.

The vulnerable bridge now runs through hedge-fund leverage, thin market depth,
private credit, life insurers, nonbank credit lines, and long-duration public
debt. A structural crash needs several of those channels to force asset sales at
the same time.

The 1929 comparison is worth keeping for one reason only. An asset-price
collapse and a credit-intermediation collapse are different events. The New York
Fed contained the immediate commercial-bank liquidity strain around the October
1929 crash. The crash still deepened a contraction that had begun in August. A
national banking panic began thirteen months later, and the banking system
collapsed in March 1933. Containing market plumbing therefore does not establish
that later credit intermediation will hold.

The AI buildout is the modern version of that distinction. The technology is
real and the aggregate payoff is unproven, and neither fact settles the question
that matters, which is how the buildout is financed. BIS names four earlier
booms, from canals to dotcom, in which a genuine breakthrough attracted more
capital than commercial returns could justify, and each ended in an investment
reversal that induced a recession. The 2000-02 episode is the useful
counterexample: real technology, real overinvestment, real capex bust, and no
banking collapse, because the losses landed on equity rather than on leveraged
intermediaries. Financing structure, not technology validity, sets severity.

The current administration changes the near-term path, but not the physical or
financial constraints. It has already used strategic oil, tanker insurance,
secondary sanctions, selective supply waivers, tariff authorities, and Treasury
buybacks. These actions can reduce an immediate recession or liquidity event.
They can also raise inflation, term premiums, allied retaliation, and policy
credibility risk.

create an Iran-only tool. Repair the unavailable geopolitical generation first,
then add a reusable shock-transmission and policy-reaction foundation. Build a
new interactive tool only if users need live counterfactual simulation that the
recurring dossier cannot express.

## Method

### Claim classification

Each material assertion was classified as one of these types:

1. Observed fact.
2. Published estimate.
3. Model inference.
4. Historical analogy.
5. Unsupported proportional extrapolation.

Every physical shortage was tested against this balance:

`net scarcity = gross disruption - accessible inventories - alternate output - rerouting - recycling - substitution - allocation priority - demand destruction`

A financial shock becomes systemic through a different chain:

`systemic damage ~= net shock x leverage x funding mismatch x forced sales - credible policy absorption`

The two balances must not be combined. A commodity can be physically tight
without causing a financial crisis. An expensive asset can fall sharply without
breaking the credit system.

### Evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| High | Current official data or direct primary-source statement. |
| Medium | Strong reporting, industry flow data, or a result with material measurement limits. |
| Low | Search discovery, an inaccessible primary table, or an inference that awaits confirmation. |

Confidence describes evidence quality. It is not the probability that an asset
will rise or that a scenario will occur.

### Verification boundary

Four distinctions govern the cycle analysis below:

- An official statement proves what an agency announced or a speaker said. It
  does not prove implementation, uptake, effectiveness, or private motive.
- A published estimate proves the authors' result under their data and model. It
  does not turn a counterfactual decomposition into an observed quantity.
- Public capex and debt data expose scale and financing direction. They do not
  reveal AI-only spend, cancellation rights, duplicated collateral, or net loss
  allocation across private contracts.
- Repeated actions can support a behavioral inference. They cannot reveal a
  closed decision rule, trigger threshold, or future commitment.

The report therefore states direct observations, source estimates, and analyst
inferences separately. A sequence in time is not treated as proof of motive.

## Physical bottleneck audit

### Oil, refined products, and Hormuz

EIA estimates that Hormuz carried 20.9 million barrels per day of oil in the
first half of 2025. That was about 20% of global liquids consumption. The route
also carried 11.4 Bcf per day of LNG. Saudi and UAE bypass pipelines provide
about 4.7 million barrels per day of capacity, so they cannot replace normal
Hormuz flow.

Current flow is uncertain. Kpler estimated crude and product flow near 2 million
barrels per day in early August. The U.S. Energy Secretary claimed almost
9 million barrels per day. Reuters found that vessel trackers saw a gap of
3 million to 5 million barrels per day versus the official claim.

On August 20, Kpler counted seven commodity ships. Four entered and three
exited. No VLCC or LNG tanker crossed in that sample. Ships with disabled
transponders were absent from the count.

The correct conclusion is severe impairment with an unusually wide measurement
band. It is not a known zero-flow state. Pipelines, Fujairah, Saudi Red Sea
exports, shuttles, U.S. shale, Venezuela, and dark voyages provide partial
offsets.

The refined-product channel is tighter than the U.S. crude balance alone. EIA
reported these U.S. stocks for the week ending August 14:

| Stock | Thousand barrels | Provenance | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Commercial crude | 428,815 | Extracted from EIA weekly series `WCESTUS1` | Available buffer, excluding the SPR. Building, not draining. |
| Motor gasoline | 209,378 | Carried forward, not re-extracted | Low enough to remain price-sensitive, but not proof of national outage. |
| Distillate | 105,619 | Carried forward, not re-extracted | The most concerning U.S. product buffer. |
| Strategic Petroleum Reserve | 293,426 | Extracted from EIA weekly series `WCSSTUS1` | Materially depleted after the emergency exchange. |

The two extracted series carry findings the headline levels hide, and the two
findings point in opposite directions. Keep them apart.

**The SPR drawdown is faster and deeper than the 2022 release.** From its 2026
plateau of 415,442 on March 20 to 293,426 on August 14, the reserve fell 122,016
thousand barrels, or 29.4%, across 21 weeks. That is an average 5.81 million
barrels per week. The comparable 2022 release ran from 593,382 on January 7 to
372,380 on December 30, a 221,002 thousand barrel decline across 51 weeks, or
4.33 million barrels per week. The current draw is running at roughly 1.34 times
the weekly pace of the largest prior release. At 293,426 the reserve sits below
every weekly reading since January 1983, about forty-three years.

**U.S. commercial crude is building, which cuts against an acute-scarcity
reading.** Commercial stocks rose from 404,508 on July 24 to 428,815 on August
14, a 24,307 thousand barrel build of 6.0% in three weeks, including a single
17,423 thousand barrel weekly jump into August 7. The August 14 level is 1.9%
above the year-ago 420,684. Emergency barrels are arriving in commercial tanks
faster than they are being consumed.

The honest synthesis is that the U.S. crude balance is currently comfortable
because a finite reserve is being spent to make it so. The scarcity has been
transferred from the spot market onto the reserve's balance sheet, where it
remains as a smaller future buffer rather than a present shortage. That is a
deferred cost, not an avoided one, and it is the single clearest instance in
this note of policy converting a visible physical shock into an invisible
optionality loss.

Global refinery throughput was nearly 5 million barrels per day below the prior
year in July. U.S. refiners and exports have offset part of that loss. Planned
maintenance and hurricane risk limit how long that offset can grow.

**Verdict:** real global oil and refined-product scarcity. The likely path is
price rationing and regional shortages, not a proven nationwide U.S. fuel
outage. Evidence grade: high for normal capacity and U.S. stocks, medium for
current Gulf flow.

BIS supplies the magnitudes that bound this shock and its policy answer. The
cut in crude flow exceeded 10 million barrels per day, about 13% of normal
global supply, against roughly 8% in the 1970s energy crises. Shut-ins have
curtailed about 12 million barrels per day from a regional supply of 21 million.
Brent rose 67% to an intraday peak of $120 in under two weeks. The IEA's 400
million barrel release, the largest in history, covered only about 20 days of
lost Hormuz flow.

Two of those facts argue for a longer tail than a price-spike framing implies.
Shut-ins can structurally damage wells, so some mature fields may never recover
pre-conflict productivity, and restarting others may not be economical. Separately,
the United States, China, Japan, and the European Union are drawing reserves at
the same time, so rebuilding them is itself a future source of demand that can
keep physical markets tight into 2027.

### Helium and semiconductors

USGS data cited by Reuters put Qatar at about 63 million cubic meters of helium
in 2025, versus roughly 190 million worldwide. Qatar therefore supplied close
to one-third of global output. The original transcript attributed the exposure
to Oman, which was incorrect.

Helium is a genuine short-cycle logistics bottleneck. It is a natural-gas
processing byproduct, liquid cargo evaporates during transport, and one industry
estimate gives users roughly 45 days after liquefaction. Spot indications
doubled after the Qatar disruption.

The downstream loss is not proportional. Suppliers prioritize MRI, aerospace,
and semiconductor users. Reuters reported an industry estimate near 95% supply
for semiconductor customers under allocation. Iwatani maintained supply through
U.S. sourcing and stockpiles. Air Liquide cited multiple continents and European
cavern storage.

Closed-loop recovery, lower-priority curtailment, alternate U.S. and Algerian
supply, and future Russian or Canadian output provide additional offsets. Those
offsets take time and do not eliminate price pressure.

**Verdict:** high-confidence price and logistics shock. Low confidence in any
claim of a one-for-one semiconductor-output loss.

The duration is now better anchored. BIS reports that damage to Qatari LNG
facilities cut capacity by 17%, with full recovery expected to take up to five
years, and that producers of the gas turbines needed for LNG compressors carry
order backlogs of three to five years. Because helium is a byproduct of
natural-gas processing, that timeline, not the shipping backlog, sets the floor
under the supply gap. BIS also confirms the demand-side framing: helium is a
small portion of the semiconductor value chain but indispensable, and major
Asian technology firms reported inventories sufficient to avoid immediate
production issues while signalling growing concern as the conflict continued.

### Sulfur, sulfuric acid, copper, nickel, and phosphate

Sulfur is one of the strongest physical bottlenecks in the audit. Refineries and
gas-processing plants produce much of it as a byproduct. A higher sulfur price
therefore cannot quickly summon equivalent new output.

The impact depends on process integration:

| Process | Exposure | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Integrated copper smelter | Lower | Generates sulfuric acid internally. |
| Non-integrated copper leach operation | High | Can reduce leach rates, buy acid, or bid sulfur away from weaker users. |
| HPAL nickel | Very high | Requires continuous acid supply and can face binary shutdown economics. |
| Phosphate fertilizer | High volume, lower margin | Cuts production when metals outbid fertilizer economics. |

Kpler reports that sulfur reached 35% to 50% of operating costs at many exposed
HPAL plants. It reports roughly 50% curtailment at one Indonesian producer.
Mosaic cut about 2 million tonnes of U.S. phosphate output. OCP shifted from
Middle East Gulf supply toward Russia and the U.S. Gulf at higher cost.

Integrated operations remain protected. DRC copper producers can often pay more
than nickel or fertilizer producers. This means the first curtailment can occur
outside copper even when copper is the headline metal.

**Verdict:** real, nonlinear, and process-specific scarcity. Evidence grade:
medium because several current flow and cost estimates come from Kpler's
commercial research.

The exposure share is now independently corroborated. BIS reports that the
Middle East accounts for close to half of seaborne sulphur, and around a third
of global seaborne exports of liquefied petroleum gas, fertilisers, and helium.
That places sulfur among the most concentrated of the affected inputs.

### Fertilizer and food

Around 40% of seaborne urea normally moves through Hormuz. The Gulf backlog
reached 25 urea-laden vessels, then unwound. Prices reached their highest level
since 2022 before easing.

Supply rerouted. Russian urea shipments to India reached about 870 thousand
tonnes in the first half, up roughly 90% year over year. Egyptian shipments to
India exceeded 600 thousand tonnes. These shifts reduced immediate scarcity but
diverted supply from other buyers.

The damage landed at planting time. Australia reduced wheat area, Argentina
deferred some planting, and U.S. corn area likely would have been higher at
lower nitrogen prices. Yield effects can continue into 2027.

USDA's August balance does not show an imminent global food shortage:

| 2026/27 world ending stocks | Million metric tons |
| --- | ---: |
| Total grains | 772.53 |
| Wheat | 273.25 |
| Coarse grains | 306.65 |
| Milled rice | 192.63 |
| Oilseeds | 146.29 |

Stocks are not equally accessible. China holds a large share of some totals,
and importing countries can still experience severe affordability shocks. A
global crisis requires a compound event such as major drought, export bans,
renewed freight interruption, or persistent fertilizer curtailment.

**Verdict:** current farm-margin, acreage, and food-price risk. No current proof
of a global caloric shortage.

Two official sources bound this in opposite directions. BIS notes that missed
planting windows cannot be recovered, that weaker harvests then constrict the
next cycle's working capital, and that the food-insecurity impact would be most
acute in low-income economies. It also records that some farmers switched from
corn to soy specifically to cut fertilizer use, which is adaptation rather than
shortage. Against that, U.S. farmland is a leveraged asset at a record price:
the Federal Reserve reports inflation-adjusted farmland prices and
price-to-rent ratios at historical highs, sustained by limited inventory despite
elevated interest rates and higher operating costs. A margin squeeze against
record collateral values is the specific configuration that turns an input-cost
shock into a credit event in the farm sector. That channel is not currently
stressed and is not in the crash ladder, but it is the one to watch if
fertilizer costs persist through a second planting cycle.

### Silver

Silver remains in structural deficit, but the size is not stable enough to use
without a date. The Silver Institute's February outlook forecast a 67 Moz 2026
deficit. Multiple summaries of the April 15 World Silver Survey report a revised
46.3 Moz forecast and a 40.3 Moz 2025 deficit.

The later estimate should supersede the preliminary number. The direct survey
PDF did not parse in this review, so the report preserves the revision as a
medium-confidence reconciliation rather than a fully verified table extraction.

Deficit does not mean immediate unavailability. Above-ground bullion, recycling,
photovoltaic thrifting, copper substitution, jewelry demand destruction, and
industrial recession all respond to price. Investment demand can move in the
opposite direction and tighten the market further.

**Verdict:** structurally tight hybrid industrial and monetary metal. It is not
a pure safe haven and not an automatic squeeze.

## Financial claim audit

### Valuation

The valuation warning is valid. The timing claim is not.

The Federal Reserve described broad equity valuations as high, forward P/E as
well above its historical median, and the equity premium as near a 20-year low.
At the August 21 close the trailing S&P 500 price-to-earnings ratio was 29.58
against a 15.08 historical median, so the index trades at 1.96 times its median
trailing multiple. CAPE was 41.96 against a 16.11 median and a 44.19 record set
in December 1999, placing it at 95% of its all-time peak. Both readings come
from a secondary aggregator of Shiller data and carry that provenance; the
Federal Reserve forward P/E and equity-premium statements are primary and should
carry the argument if the two ever disagree.

Proximity to the 1999 CAPE record is a statement about the starting point for
long-horizon returns, not about timing. CAPE sat above 40 for much of 1998 and
1999 before the drawdown arrived, so "near the record" has historically been
compatible with another year of gains.

CAPE smooths ten years of real earnings. It is useful for long-horizon return
expectations, but wars, tax changes, sector composition, buybacks, and profit
shares alter its comparability. It does not identify a crash date.

The Buffett indicator has an additional denominator problem. U.S.-listed firms
earn globally, while U.S. GDP measures domestic production. Intangibles,
foreign listings, buybacks, private-company share, and shifting profit margins
also change the ratio. This audit therefore does not use a single Buffett
indicator level as a timing signal.

Two structural facts make a given percentage decline matter more than it once
did. Concentration is extreme: BIS reports that the largest 30 stocks in the
S&P 500 represented 56% of index value on March 26, 2026, and that U.S. stocks
are about 64% of the MSCI Global index. Household exposure is also larger, with
U.S. equity wealth relative to disposable income more than doubling since 2010.
A correction of the same size therefore produces a larger wealth effect and a
sharper consumption pullback than the historical average, and a U.S.-led
repricing propagates globally.

The actual 2026 shock is smaller than the rhetoric around it. Global equities
fell 9% from late February to end-March 2026 and the S&P 500 fell 8%. BIS places
that against the 2025 tariff episode at 19%, the 1990 Gulf War at 17%, and the
1979 Iranian revolution at 17%. Credit spreads widened and then compressed to
levels low by historical standards. The gap between a historically large
physical supply shock and a contained market response is the single most
important unexplained fact in this audit. It is consistent with either a
well-absorbed shock or with risk that has not yet been priced.

Market liquidity behaved the same way. Treasury liquidity deteriorated in March
with Middle East volatility, then recovered, leaving depth little changed on
net. Depth for the most liquid on-the-run two-year note remains near the first
quartile of its history, and a measure of equity-market liquidity moved toward
the lower end of its distribution since 2019. The term premium rose to near the
top of its 15-year range while remaining in line with its longer-run median.
The system was tested at step 1 of the crash ladder and held.

### Margin debt and market structure

FINRA reported $1.502 trillion of customer margin debit in June and
$1.417 trillion in July. July was about 38.6% above July 2025. That leverage can
accelerate liquidation after an equity decline.

The monthly direction matters as much as the annual level. June was the series
peak. July fell $84.8 billion from it, a 5.6% monthly decline, the first
meaningful reversal in a run that had risen almost without interruption from
$1.023 trillion in July 2025. Rising margin debt is a vulnerability. Falling
margin debt from a peak is either healthy de-risking or the early phase of the
liquidation the vulnerability describes. This series cannot distinguish the two,
and a single month is not a trend. It should be read alongside market depth,
not alone.

Passive ownership is not a one-directional crash machine. BIS research finds
that index trading can increase constituent comovement and reduce
security-specific price information. ETF arbitrage creates an extra transmission
channel. The same research finds passive mutual-fund flows more stable than
active-fund flows during several stress episodes.

The net effect depends on investor behavior, market depth, derivatives, and
authorized-participant capacity. Passive structure can amplify some moves. It
does not independently create a systemic event.

### Housing and banks

The 2008 analogy fails at the household and bank core:

- Household debt-to-GDP is at more than 25-year lows.
- Mortgage leverage remains far below previous peaks.
- Mortgage delinquency ticked up in 2025 Q4 but stayed at the lower end of its
  historical range.
- Very few homeowners have negative equity.
- Credit standards for mortgage borrowers remain tight relative to the early
  2000s, so elevated house prices are not a weak-underwriting artifact.
- Bank CET1 ratios remain near historically high levels.
- Bank HQLA relative to short-term debt is near the high end of history.
- Uninsured deposits remain well below the 2022-23 peak.

Risks remain, and they should be named precisely rather than bundled. Bank
securities still carried about $300 billion of fair-value losses at the end of
2025. Auto delinquency sits at the 90th percentile of its historical
distribution, and used-car loan maturities at origination remain near record
length, which raises the chance of deep negative equity. Credit-card
delinquencies moved the other way: they edged down in 2025 Q4 and leveled off at
their long-term median, though they remain elevated against the past ten years.
Some distress is concentrated among recent low-down-payment buyers, especially
FHA and VA borrowers. Commercial real estate refinancing can create localized
losses. None of this recreates the broad weak-underwriting and negative-equity
mechanism of 2008.

One fact cuts against a credit-crunch reading and belongs here for balance.
Banks eased lending standards again in 2025 Q4, and eased commercial real estate
standards through the second half of 2025. Delinquency rates on bank loans
declined across key categories. A system in the early stage of a credit
contraction does not usually look like this. The corporate picture is less
reassuring: gross leverage at publicly traded nonfinancial firms edged down but
remains high by its own history, and the median interest coverage ratio for
non-investment-grade firms sits in the bottom quartile of its distribution.
Investment-grade borrowers, at nearly 70% of public nonfinancial debt, remain
robust. Privately held firms carry roughly 60% of all U.S. nonfinancial business
debt, and their riskier leveraged-loan and private-credit borrowings are about
10% of the total.

### Hedge funds, private credit, and life insurers

The systemic weak points have moved outside traditional bank balance sheets.

The Federal Reserve reported hedge-fund gross leverage at record-high but stable
levels. The composition matters more than the level. A decrease in the
cash-futures basis trade was largely offset by other relative-value trades such
as swap-spread trades, so the exposure rotated rather than shrank. Large funds
hold important Treasury, rate-derivative, and equity positions, and dealer
intermediation in Treasury markets grew specifically through repo lending to
hedge-fund clients. A funding withdrawal can therefore force sales into already
thin markets. Dealer value-at-risk stayed below internal limits, which is the
reason this has not yet happened, and also the reason it could happen quickly if
volatility pushes value-at-risk toward those limits.

Private credit reached about $1.4 trillion. It represents roughly 10% of U.S.
nonfinancial corporate debt and about one-third of below-investment-grade debt,
excluding bank loans. Semi-liquid vehicles held about $425 billion of gross
assets and $241 billion of net assets, roughly 20% of net assets under
management in private-credit vehicles. Perpetual-life BDCs account for $306
billion gross and $161 billion net; interval funds account for $119 billion
gross and $80 billion net.

Redemption requests rose sharply in late 2025 and accelerated in 2026 Q1, when a
number of funds received requests much larger than 5% of net asset value. Most
perpetual BDCs capped redemptions at that 5% level. One threshold was crossed:
accepted redemptions somewhat exceeded new inflows in 2026 Q1, the first time
this has happened since these vehicles were created. The Federal Reserve still
judges the risk limited and manageable, because for the largest 10 perpetual
BDCs, holding 80% of sector assets, available bank credit and cash can cover at
least three calendar quarters of net redemptions at the 5% cap. Its warning is
about credit availability rather than about a run: persistent outflows reduce
credit to riskier borrowers who cannot easily replace it.

The reason sentiment turned is now documented rather than inferred, and it links
this section directly to the AI cycle. The Federal Reserve attributes
deteriorating private-credit sentiment to high-profile corporate defaults and to
concerns that advances in AI will disrupt industries, particularly software.
Software had become the largest sector in private-credit portfolios, driven by
private equity activity. The same concentration shows up in prices: secondary
leveraged-loan spreads rose because of the high concentration of borrowers in
software, spreads for speculative-grade technology firms widened more notably
than the market, and new-loan spreads in private credit ticked up on credit
quality concerns.

Core private funds have structural strengths. Matvos, Piskorski, and Seru,
using about 1,300 funds and nearly 9,000 loan holdings covering roughly 65% of
U.S. private credit activity, find equity at 65% to 80% of the financial
structure against about 10% for commercial banks. The equity-to-assets ratio
averages 0.85, and 0.79 asset-weighted, so leverage does not rise with size.
Even among funds using bank credit lines, about two-thirds of assets remain
equity financed. Maturity mismatch is minimal: funds live about ten years while
underlying loans mature in about five, so asset cash flows arrive before the
fund winds down. Realized losses land on equity holders, with mean annualized
net returns to limited partners of 9.6% and a 5th-percentile return near
negative 3.8%.

Those authors attach their own warning, and it should be quoted rather than
softened. Most of the sector's expansion happened under benign credit
conditions, so its resilience under severe macroeconomic stress remains
untested. They name competitive pressure toward higher leverage and looser
underwriting, expanding bank-nonbank linkages in downturns, transparency, and
correlated adjustment by limited partners who hold many funds at once. They also
name valuation contagion, in which uncertainty about asset values spreads into
fundraising, secondary markets, and affiliated vehicles.

The vulnerable links are semi-liquid wrappers, opacity, software concentration,
payment-in-kind interest, correlated limited partners, and bank credit lines.
Private-credit loan defaults remain low, but elevated use of payment-in-kind
provisions indicates some borrowers may face repayment difficulty. Bank
commitments to all nonbank financial institutions reached $2.6 trillion. Private
equity, BDCs, and private credit were the largest category at about 25% of that
total, growing 17% year over year against 14% for all nonbank commitments.

Life-insurer leverage remains in the upper historical quartile. Illiquid assets
were about 37% of life-insurer assets in 2024, against 14% for property and
casualty insurers. Nontraditional liabilities reached $531 billion in late 2025,
growing 15% in real terms over the year while remaining small relative to total
assets. This creates another channel between private credit, funding markets,
and long-duration assets.

One live test has already been run and passed. When the Iran conflict hit energy
markets, central counterparties raised margin requirements significantly for
crude oil and European natural gas, and there was no evidence that participants
struggled to meet those calls. That is direct evidence against step 2 of the
crash ladder under the shock actually delivered.

**Verdict:** a credible nonbank deleveraging mechanism exists. It is not yet an
observed system-wide run.

### 1929: crash versus credit-system break

The 1929 analogy is used constantly and almost always incorrectly. The useful
lesson is not that an expensive market fell. It is that an asset-price collapse
and a credit-intermediation collapse are different events with different
mechanisms, different durations, and different policy answers.

**The contraction preceded the crash.** The Great Depression began in August
1929, when the expansion of the 1920s ended. The Dow peaked at 381.17 on
September 3. Black Monday, October 28, took the index down nearly 13%, and
Black Tuesday another 12%.

**The crash deepened the contraction but did not explain the later credit-system
break.** It transmitted through wealth loss, fear, and deferred durable-goods
purchases bought on credit. Firms such as Ford saw demand fall, cut production,
and furloughed workers. Federal Reserve History says this direct impact faded
within months. A named minority view, held by Meltzer among others, treats the
crash as a symptom of the 1929 contraction rather than a contributing force.

**The immediate financial crisis was contained by policy.** The New York Fed
bought government securities, expedited discount lending, lowered its discount
rate, and told commercial banks it would supply the reserves they needed. The
action was controversial at the time and the Board complained New York had
exceeded its authority. In hindsight it worked: the market collapsed, but the
commercial banks nearest the storm stayed open. By the fall of 1930 recovery
appeared imminent.

**A national banking panic began thirteen months later. The system-wide collapse
came in March 1933.** The prior three contractions had averaged fifteen months,
and the 1929 downturn had run fifteen months. Then Caldwell and Company
collapsed. The Bank of Tennessee closed November 7, 1930. Knoxville and
Louisville affiliates followed on November 12 and 17. A correspondent cascade
forced hundreds of banks to suspend. On December 11 the Bank of United States,
the fourth-largest in New York City, ceased operations.

Two pre-existing design flaws converted a regional failure into a national one.
Banks counted checks in the process of collection as cash reserves, so the same
cash appeared on two balance sheets. Bankers called these fictitious reserves,
and their quantity peaked just before the 1930 crisis. Separately, the reserve
pyramid meant a country bank needing cash had to ask a correspondent that might
itself be under run or holding only checks in the mail.

**The transmission chain, in order:** deposit withdrawal, reserve hoarding, bank
failure, destruction of lender-specific borrower information, credit rationing,
deflation, and debt-burden amplification. Bernanke's contribution is the
information channel: when a bank failed, what it knew about who in its community
was a good credit risk disappeared, so firms paid more for working capital and
some could not borrow at any price. Friedman and Schwartz supply the monetary
channel: hoarding and reserve accumulation shrank the money stock.

**The magnitudes.** From the fall of 1930 through the winter of 1933 the money
supply fell by nearly 30%, and average prices fell by an equivalent amount.
Mitchener and Richardson estimate that the 1929-32 panics reduced lending by 13%
relative to its 1929 value, and reduced the money multiplier and money supply by
36%. They attribute about 41% of the decline in bank lending and about
nine-tenths of the decline in the money multiplier to the panics themselves.

The money-stock and price declines are historical measurements. The 13%, 36%,
41%, and nine-tenths shares are econometric counterfactual estimates. They must
remain attributed to Mitchener and Richardson rather than presented as directly
observed decompositions.

**Do not tell a pure irrational-run story.** Calomiris and Mason find that
fundamentals explain most of the incidence of bank failure, and that contagion
or liquidity crises were a relatively unimportant influence on failure risk
before 1933. The first two Friedman-Schwartz crises, in 1930 and 1931, show no
positive unexplained residual failure risk. The third is ambiguous, and even
treating it as a genuine national liquidity crisis bounds the contagion effect
as small. Only the last crisis, at the beginning of 1933, is associated with
important unexplained increases in failure risk. They also find some regional
and local contagion, but of small aggregate importance. The depression mechanism
was weak assets, runs, deflation, a fragmented dual banking system, and policy
failure acting together.

The two studies do not estimate the same object. Mitchener and Richardson
estimate panic effects on lending and monetary aggregates. Calomiris and Mason
model which banks failed. Their results can coexist, and neither identifies one
sufficient cause for the full Depression path.

**The strongest policy evidence in this source set is a quasi-experiment.** The
1930 crisis hit two Federal Reserve districts with opposite
doctrines. Atlanta's leaders believed lender-of-last-resort duty extended to the
broader banking system: they expedited discount lending, encouraged member banks
to lend to nonmember respondents, and rushed funds to towns under panic. St.
Louis took the narrower view and refused to rediscount for nonmember
accommodation. In the Sixth District the contraction slowed and recovery began.
In the Eighth, hundreds of banks failed, lending declined, business faltered, and
unemployment rose. The border design supports a policy effect under local
comparability assumptions. It does not prove that policy alone explains every
difference between the districts or the national Depression.

**Policy capacity was contested, not absent.** The Fed's own framework worked
against it. Real-bills doctrine implied contracting credit during contractions;
its liquidationist variant, advocated by Treasury Secretary Mellon, held that
central banks should stand aside so weak institutions would fail. In the spring
of 1931 the Fed began expanding the monetary base, but not enough to offset the
panics. In the spring of 1932, once Congress granted the authority, it expanded
aggressively, the policy appeared to work, and then after a few months the Fed
reversed course and the contraction resumed. Partial success at one moment did
not produce a durable restoration.

**The distinction that matters for 2026:** in this episode, the crash's direct
effects faded faster than the later intermediation damage. The 1929 lesson is
not "expensive markets crash." It is that containing the market event does not
prove that the credit system will hold thirteen months later.

## AI, productivity, and labor

AI has strong task-specific evidence and weak realized macro evidence. Both can
be true.

An NBER summary of nearly 6,000 senior executives across the United States,
United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia found 69% of businesses reporting some
current AI use, highest in the United States at 78% and lowest in Australia at
59%. More than 90% reported no employment effect over the prior three years, and
89% reported no labor-productivity effect. Among the minority reporting any
effect, the tilt is slightly positive, with an estimated average boost of 0.29%.
That qualifier is load-bearing. The number describes the firms that saw
something, not the average firm.

Use intensity is the other reason the adoption headline overstates the change.
More than two-thirds of senior executives use AI in a typical week, but for
about 1.5 hours. Surveyed U.S. employees averaged about 1.8 hours. Broad, thin
use is what 69% adoption currently means.

That 69% must not be read as a U.S. business-population adoption rate. The
Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey, as charted by the Federal Reserve,
puts the share of U.S. businesses reporting AI use in the prior two weeks at
19.8% as of mid-June 2026, up from 17.3% in late December 2025. Adoption is
strongly graded by size, reaching 38.5% for the largest employment bucket
against roughly 19% for the smallest. The two surveys measure different
populations and ask different questions. Cite them separately.

Sectoral productivity also diverges by AI exposure, but the divergence is not
clean causal evidence. In 2026 Q1 the Federal Reserve's high-exposure grouping,
covering information, finance, and professional and business services, grew
labor productivity at 1.49% against 0.45% for medium exposure and 0.17% for low.
The same series shows high-exposure sectors leading before generative AI
existed, so part of the gap is a pre-existing sector effect rather than an AI
effect. BIS reports the same pattern with the same caution, finding higher
productivity gains in AI-exposed U.S. sectors partly at the expense of lower
employment growth.

Executives expect a 1.4% productivity gain and a 0.7% employment decline over
the next three years. About two-thirds of the expected workforce reduction comes
from reduced hiring, not direct layoffs.

Those expectations are contested by the people being forecast about. Surveyed
U.S. workers expect AI to raise employment at their firms by about 0.5% over
three years, while U.S. executives predict a 1.2% reduction. Workers expect
productivity gains of 0.9% against the executives' 2.3%. Neither group has
privileged information about an unrealized future. A forward number on which the
two sides of the same firm disagree by roughly 1.7 percentage points on
employment should not be treated as a forecast.

A separate survey of nearly 750 corporate executives supplies the mechanism and
a caution. Baslandze and coauthors find productivity gains that are positive,
sector-varying, concentrated in high-skill services and finance, and driven by
revenue-based total factor productivity rather than capital deepening. They also
document a productivity paradox in which perceived gains exceed measured gains,
which they attribute to delayed revenue realization. They find little evidence of
near-term aggregate employment decline, alongside compositional reallocation:
routine clerical roles decline while demand for skilled technical roles rises.

BLS reported second-quarter nonfarm productivity up 1.4% annualized and 2.2%
year over year. Real hourly compensation fell 3.1% in the quarter. Labor's share
fell to 52.9%, the lowest in the series beginning in 1947.

The cycle context prevents the most tempting misreading. Over the current
business cycle, starting in 2019 Q4, labor productivity has grown at a 2.1%
annualized rate. That is above the 1.5% rate of the 2007-2019 cycle and exactly
equal to the 2.1% long-term rate since 1947. Productivity is running at its
historical average, not above it. Nothing in the aggregate series yet requires
an AI explanation.

July payrolls fell 23,000. The prior two months were revised down by 103,000.
Participation was 61.4%, down 0.7 percentage point since January. The
unemployment rate remained 4.1%.

The evidence supports three simultaneous claims:

1. Productivity is growing at its long-run average rate, not above it.
2. Labor-market breadth and worker income are weak.
3. AI has not yet delivered enough realized economy-wide output to justify any
   valuation or eliminate recession risk.

The Federal Reserve staff note reaches the same conclusion and should be quoted
rather than paraphrased upward: the evidence is consistent with a buildout phase
rather than the onset of broad-based displacement. Capabilities advance,
investment booms, adoption rises, and labor-market effects remain concentrated.
The authors name what would change that reading, and this note adopts their
tests: a sustained change in labor-market dynamics among AI-exposed groups,
capital expenditure becoming associated with measured productivity gains, or a
widening gap between high- and low-exposure sectoral productivity growth. The
note carries a standard disclaimer that its analysis does not indicate Board of
Governors concurrence.

## Technology investment and financing cycle

A genuine general-purpose technology and a commercially excessive investment
cycle are not alternatives. They routinely occur together. Separating the four
questions below prevents the most common analytical error, which is treating a
verdict on the technology as a verdict on the capital structure financing it.

1. Is the technology real?
2. Will these specific projects earn their cost of capital?
3. How is the buildout financed, and what happens if returns disappoint?
4. Does the buildout arrive before or after the demand it assumes?

BIS states the historical pattern directly. The canal mania of the 1830s, the
British railway mania of the 1840s, the electrification exuberance of the late
1920s, and the dotcom boom of the late 1990s shared one trait: a genuine
technological breakthrough that attracted capital in excess of what commercial
returns could ultimately justify. Each ended with an investment reversal that
induced an economy-wide recession. The roaring-twenties entry is the bridge
between this section and the 1929 analysis above. The same decade produced both
a real electrification boom and the credit structure that later failed.

### State sequence

Innovation, then adoption expectations, then capex acceleration, then
bottlenecks and crowding, then the return-on-investment test, then financing
repricing, then capex cuts, then supplier cash-flow stress, then either credit
contraction or adaptation.

A market correction is an observable trigger inside this sequence. It is not the
mechanism. The mechanism is whether financing repricing propagates into
intermediation failure.

### Where 2026 sits

A Federal Reserve staff note characterizes the current period as a buildout
phase rather than broad aggregate displacement. Two of its series must be kept
separate:

| Series | Latest reading | Scope caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private data-center construction, annualized | $59.31 billion in May 2026, from $58.12 billion in March | Structures only. Excludes the servers installed inside them. |
| Private nonresidential investment in computers and peripheral equipment, annualized | $384.18 billion in 2026 Q1 | Equipment only, and includes substantial non-AI activity. |

These are different panels measuring different things. Adding them produces a
number that describes nothing. Neither is an "AI total."

Hyperscaler capital expenditure is likewise not an AI-specific measure. The
Federal Reserve's series is total purchases of property and equipment from 10-Q
filings, which reached roughly $156 billion across the tracked firms in 2026 Q1
alone. The Federal Reserve supplies the cleanest available scope check on that
number: as of year-end 2025, only about one-third of Amazon's gross property,
plant, and equipment balance consisted of servers and networking equipment, and
Amazon is consistently the largest spender in the series. Treating headline
hyperscaler capex as AI spending therefore overstates it. The Federal Reserve
also notes the error can run the other way, because hyperscalers increasingly
lease data-center capacity, which the capex line does not capture.

BIS reports that the five largest U.S. hyperscalers are set to spend over
$1 trillion on AI-related capital expenditure across 2025 and 2026, that these
commitments outpace their earnings and free cash flow, and that some are issuing
debt as a result. The 2026 capex values are forecasts collected from earnings
calls and press releases, not realized audited AI-only spending. The BIS annual
report also cites estimates that AI-related investment contributed roughly one
percentage point to U.S. real GDP growth in 2025.

The growth contribution is smaller than gross investment implies, because much
of the equipment is imported. The Federal Reserve's own decomposition for 2026
Q1 puts software at 0.53 percentage point and computers and peripherals at 0.59,
against a drag of 0.45 from net exports of computers, peripherals, and parts.
Any analysis that counts gross AI investment without the import offset
overstates the domestic growth contribution.

### Why competition can make it worse

BIS identifies a contest mechanism that is absent from most bubble commentary.
The investment race is partly driven by the belief that only a few players will
ultimately dominate. Under that belief, competitive pressure drives capex higher
than any single firm would choose, and the sector's net economic surplus, the
total payoff less investment costs, declines and can turn negative in adverse
scenarios. Rational firms competing for a winner-take-most prize can therefore
destroy value in aggregate even when the underlying technology delivers.

Supply constraints can amplify rather than damp this. Bottlenecks in
electricity, advanced semiconductors, and grid equipment push firms to lock in
future capacity through long-dated contracts, which increases their exposure to
any demand disappointment.

### The scenario most forecasts omit

BIS models four long-run AI outcomes, and the fourth is absent from almost all
commentary. Business as usual holds trend growth at about 2%. A bounded
productivity boost shifts trend permanently, which has not happened since the
Industrial Revolution. Transformative AI produces accelerating growth and drives
the labour share toward zero.

The fourth is the demand bottleneck. Automation diverts income from labour,
which is spent on goods and services, into further AI investment. Every
displaced worker is also a lost consumer. Forward-looking firms, seeing a
shrinking future market for AI-produced output, stop finding it profitable to
automate the next task. Output growth rises first and then falls below its
historical trend. Productivity stalls for want of demand rather than for want of
technology.

This scenario matters here because it inverts the inflation conclusion. Under
the bounded-boost and transformative cases, higher productivity growth raises
the marginal product of capital and lifts the natural rate, which is
inflationary on net. Under the demand bottleneck, the natural rate initially
rises and then falls below its pre-AI baseline, and medium-term pressure turns
disinflationary. An AI-driven stagflation forecast and an AI-driven
disinflation forecast can both be internally consistent. They differ on whether
innovation rents depend on consumer demand, which is not observable today.

BIS is explicit that the transformative case remains a possibility rather than a
present reality, because economies currently operate in a regime where tasks are
strong complements and the slowest-improving task constrains output. This note
therefore treats any single AI macro path as unforecastable and prices the
transmission mechanics instead.

### The financing seam

The authors of BIS Bulletin 120 judge the macro-financial stability risks from
the AI boom to be moderate. They tie sustainability to whether AI firms meet
high earnings expectations. Their sharpest observation is a cross-asset
divergence that is already visible rather than hypothetical: equity prices have
run far ahead of debt-market pricing, and the credit spreads of some AI firms
have already begun to widen even as equity markets continue to price significant
upside.

Three opacity features compound the exposure:

- Circular financing. Chip makers and hyperscalers take equity stakes in AI labs
  or neocloud providers, which then commit to multi-year purchases of chips or
  compute.
- Outsourced construction. Data-center building is increasingly done by third
  parties that lease facilities back on long-dated contracts with embedded exit
  clauses, poorly disclosed, with the risk of the same asset being pledged more
  than once.
- Weak supplier balance sheets. Engineering, procurement, and construction
  contractors are comparatively weak and are directly exposed to any hyperscaler
  capex pullback.

Private credit concentration is measurable. BIS reports that direct lending
funds have quadrupled their lending to AI and IT sectors over five years to
about 15% of their portfolios, that these loans are larger than their other
loans while carrying broadly similar tenor and pricing, and that software firms
increasingly draw on multiple private-credit lenders at once.

The Federal Reserve corroborates the seam from the U.S. side, and its evidence
runs in both directions. Bond issuance by the largest investment-grade firms
involved in cloud computing neared $100 billion in 2026 Q1 and met strong
investor demand, which is the shift from cash flow to debt happening in public
markets at scale and without stress. At the same time, spreads for
speculative-grade technology firms widened more notably than the market,
secondary leveraged-loan spreads rose on software concentration, and
private-credit new-loan spreads ticked up on credit-quality concerns. The
investment-grade core is funding itself easily. The speculative-grade and
privately financed periphery is already repricing. That split, not the aggregate
capex number, is where this cycle will be decided.

| Evidence | What it supports | What remains unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |

These signals justify monitoring a financing transition. They do not establish
an AI bubble, insolvency, fraud, or an imminent credit contraction. Public data
show exposure and repricing. They do not reveal the closed contract network that
would determine losses after a capex cancellation.

### Modern credit-transmission ladder

This is the 2026 analogue of the 1930 chain. Each step is observable.

1. AI return disappointment, or higher long rates.
2. Technology equity repricing.
3. Hyperscaler capex reduction.
4. Revenue loss at chip, power, cooling, construction, EPC, and data-center
   counterparties.
5. Repricing of circular financing and long-dated contracts.
6. Private-credit deterioration and redemption pressure.
7. Draws on bank credit lines and tighter dealer financing.
8. Hedge-fund or repo deleveraging.
9. Wider corporate spreads.
10. Lower investment, hiring, and consumption.
11. Policy restoration, or further contraction.

Steps 1 and 2 are conditional. Step 5 is not the same mechanism as the 1930
loss of lender-specific borrower information. It is a proposed opacity channel.
Poorly disclosed contracts and possible multiple pledges could make collateral
hard to value or finance when confidence breaks. Public sources establish the
opacity and the risk, not duplicate pledging at system-wide scale. The channel
becomes observable only if margins rise, lenders reject collateral, or
refinancing fails.

### Five-episode comparison

| Episode | Technology | Financing structure | Intermediation outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1929-33 | Real electrification and industrial technology | Margin leverage, fragmented dual banking, gold-standard constraint | Full credit-system break, debt deflation, money supply down nearly 30% |
| 2000-02 | Real internet and networking technology | Largely equity-financed overinvestment | Capex recession without broad banking collapse |
| 2008-09 | Not a technology cycle | Housing collateral, bank and shadow-bank leverage | Insolvency and frozen wholesale funding |
| 2020 | Not a technology cycle | Exogenous shutdown, deflationary demand shock | Averted by unusually broad fiscal and monetary capacity |
| 2026 | Real AI capability, unproven aggregate payoff | Concentrated valuations, rising debt reliance, opaque private credit, circular financing | Undetermined. Core banks strong, nonbank leverage growing, inflation and thin fiscal space constrain response |

The 2000-02 row is the most instructive counterexample. The technology was
real, the overinvestment was real, the capex bust was real, and there was still
no banking collapse, because the losses landed on equity holders rather than on
leveraged intermediaries. Financing structure, not technology validity,
determined the severity.

### Policy limits

Five constraints keep this reaction function from eliminating downside:

1. The SPR is already depleted and cannot replace normal Hormuz capacity.
2. Tariffs and sanctions can add inflation while they pursue strategic goals.
3. Treasury buybacks address liquidity, not debt sustainability.
4. The Federal Reserve remains a separate institution with an inflation mandate.
5. Allies, China, Iran, shipowners, insurers, and markets can refuse the intended
   response.

The fiscal constraint deserves a number rather than an adjective. BIS reports
that cyclically adjusted primary deficits in advanced economies averaged 1.9% of
GDP from 2022 onward, nearly double the 1.1% of the two preceding decades, and
that emerging-market deficits deteriorated further, from 0.1% to 1.8%. The
decisive change is arithmetic: the gap between bond yields and nominal GDP
growth, previously deeply negative, has turned positive in many countries.
Growth no longer stabilizes debt ratios on its own. Governments must now run
primary surpluses or much smaller deficits simply to hold debt-to-GDP flat. That
makes a 2020-style fiscal response more costly and less sustainable. It does not
make one legally or operationally unavailable.

The tariff record also limits the third constraint. Statutory and effective
tariff rates diverged sharply: Gopinath and Neiman find the September 2025
trade-weighted statutory rate at 27.4% against an actual rate paid of 14.1%,
roughly half, because of shipping lags, exemptions, a jump in USMCA utilization
from below 50% to nearly 90%, and uneven enforcement. Announced tariff policy
and realized tariff incidence are different quantities. Forecasts built on
announced rates have overstated the shock by about a factor of two.

### Policy layers

Policy is not one capability. It is a stack, and each layer answers a different
failure. Success at one layer is not evidence that the next layer is healthy.

| Layer | Instrument | Failure it addresses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market plumbing | Circuit breakers, trading halts | Disorderly price discovery |
| Liquidity | Repo operations, discount window, swap lines | Funding shortage at solvent institutions |
| Market intermediation | Credit and dealer facilities | Impaired market making and credit flow |
| Solvency | Recapitalization, resolution | Insolvent institutions |
| Income and demand | Fiscal transfers | Aggregate demand collapse |
| Physical scarcity | Reserve releases, supply and allocation policy | Real shortage of a good |

The 1929 sequence is the cleanest demonstration. The New York Fed executed the
liquidity layer well in October 1929 and contained the immediate bank crisis.
That success said nothing about the solvency and lender-of-last-resort layers,
which failed thirteen months later and produced the Depression. The spring 1932
episode repeats the lesson within the monetary layer alone: aggressive base
expansion appeared to work, then the Fed reversed course and the contraction
resumed.

Two constraints apply the lesson to 2026. First, physical scarcity has no
monetary answer. BIS notes the IEA's 400 million barrel release, the largest in
history, covered only about 20 days of lost Hormuz flow. Second, the layers can
conflict. The Federal Reserve could support market functioning through liquidity
operations while declining to cut policy rates, because inflation constrains the
rate instrument but not the plumbing instrument. Reading a liquidity
intervention as a signal of imminent easing would be a category error. This is
an institutional capability, not a forecast. A broad Section 13(3) facility
would require prior approval from the Treasury Secretary.

These numbers have not moved, and the reason should be explicit rather than
implied. Re-verification produced evidence in both directions, and none of it is
a confirmed state transition.

Against the crash tail: central counterparties raised margins during the energy
shock and every call was met; Treasury liquidity deteriorated in March and then

key categories; credit-card delinquencies leveled off at their long-term median;
household debt-to-GDP sits at more than 25-year lows; the largest perpetual BDCs

## Beneficiaries and protection by regime

No public company is a true safe haven. Equity beneficiaries are relative
survivors with operating, valuation, and policy risk.

| Regime | Relative beneficiaries | Main losers | Better protection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Managed adaptation | Grid equipment, efficient automation, cyber defense, selective energy infrastructure, quality industrials, and firms with pricing power. | Businesses that cannot fund capex or pass through input costs. | Diversified quality equities plus short government bills. |
| Stagflationary correction | Energy and refined-product exposure, allocated gold, TIPS held to maturity, logistics efficiency, and low-leverage pricing power. | Long nominal duration, unhedged transports, low-margin consumers, and levered real estate. | T-bills, TIPS, allocated gold, and pre-funded commodity hedges. |
| Ordinary recession | High-quality balance sheets, essential services, cash-rich firms, and duration after inflation clearly breaks. | Cyclicals, lower-quality credit, private-software borrowers, and small firms dependent on refinancing. | T-bills first, then longer high-quality duration after disinflation confirms. |
| Structural crash | Exchanges after the initial liquidation, restructuring capacity, selected market infrastructure, and eventual policy-supported quality assets. | Leveraged funds, illiquid credit, crowded equities, weak insurers, and funding-dependent businesses. | Government money funds, T-bills, USD liquidity, insured deposits, and pre-funded hedges. |
| Rescue and reflation after a crash | Gold, quality growth, grid and energy investment, and miners after balance-sheet repair. | Cash held too long, weak nominal creditors, and firms diluted during rescue. | Rotate only after funding markets and policy implementation confirm. |

Gold can fall during the first liquidity scramble. Long Treasuries can fail as a
hedge when inflation or fiscal credibility drives the shock. Silver can trade as
an industrial metal during recession. Protection must match the phase.

## What would turn a correction into a structural crash

Watch the transmission chain, not the headline index level. Each step below
names the intermediation state that must break, not the price that must fall.

| Step | Intermediation state | Confirming observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Market depth degrades | Treasury or equity depth falls while volatility and margins rise |
| 2 | Funding withdrawn from leveraged holders | Hedge funds lose repo or derivative financing and sell common positions |
| 3 | Redemption channel jams | Private-credit gates persist while bank credit lines are drawn |
| 4 | Forced sale of illiquid assets | Life insurers or funds sell into weak secondary markets |
| 5 | Refinancing market closes | Corporate refinancing fails outside the weakest credits |
| 6 | Bank intermediation constrained | Bank capital or liquidity falls enough to reduce credit supply |
| 7 | Policy restoration fails | Facilities do not restore function, or inflation blocks their use |

Today, steps 1 through 3 have partial evidence. Steps 4 through 7 are not
system-wide observed facts.

The 1929 mapping is an analytical analogy, not an exact historical identity.
October 1929 reached step 1, and the New York Fed contained the immediate bank
liquidity strain. The November 1930 panic began to impair step 6. Repeated panics
and policy failures culminated in the March 1933 banking collapse. A market
plumbing event can remain contained. A persistent intermediation failure can
turn a contraction into a depression.

## Unresolved questions and limits

- The current Hormuz flow estimate has a multi-million-barrel measurement gap.
  Arrival data can narrow it after voyage lags.
- U.S. commercial crude and SPR levels are extracted from the EIA weekly series
  and are current. Motor gasoline and distillate are carried forward and have
  not been extracted from a primary series; treat those two rows as the weakest
  numbers in the physical section.
- The April silver survey PDF did not parse. The 46.3 Moz revision is supported
  by multiple summaries, but the exact table still needs direct extraction.
- CAPE and trailing price-to-earnings are current as of the August 21 close but
  come from a secondary aggregator of Shiller data. The Federal Reserve forward
  price-to-earnings and equity-premium statements are primary and carry the
  argument where the two disagree.
- Current copper and sulfur estimates rely partly on Kpler commercial research.
  BIS now corroborates the Middle East share of seaborne sulphur, but company
  disclosures and official production data should confirm specific curtailments.
- The 0.29% AI productivity figure is a conditional mean over firms reporting an
  effect. The underlying working paper should be read directly to determine
  whether an unconditional sample mean exists.
- Policy statements are not implementation. Sanctions, fiscal consolidation,
  and reserve repayment require observed execution.
- The scenario probabilities are judgmental. They are not trained-model output
  or market-implied odds.
- The largest unexplained fact in this note is the gap between a historically
  large physical supply shock and a contained market response. It is consistent
  with a well-absorbed shock and with unpriced risk, and this audit cannot
  currently distinguish them.
- Public filings do not disclose a complete AI-only capex total, contract exit
  rights, duplicate collateral, or net private counterparty exposure. The
  financing ladder is therefore a conditional transmission model, not a map of
  known losses.
- The action record does not reveal private Trump, Bessent, Treasury, DFC, or
  Federal Reserve trigger thresholds. Forecasts in the reaction-function table
  infer behavior from repeated public actions and remain falsifiable.
- Historical panic shares are model-based counterfactual estimates. The
  underlying literature supports multiple interacting causes rather than one
  sufficient crash or panic mechanism.
- Aggregate U.S. resilience can coexist with severe regional, sector, and
  household distress.
