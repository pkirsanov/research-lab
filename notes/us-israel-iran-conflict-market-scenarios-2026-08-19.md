# U.S.-Israel-Iran Conflict, Commodity, And Equity Scenario Review

Research snapshot as of 2026-08-19. This is a dated cross-asset research note.
It is not a live signal, a portfolio, personalized advice, or an instruction to
trade. Every market level, probability, and move range must be refreshed before
reuse.

## Bottom line

- The conflict is already a direct U.S.-Israeli war with Iran, not merely a risk
  of future war. The near-term question is whether the current standoff becomes
  a wider multi-front campaign.
- Iran's most likely escalation path is distributed pressure on U.S. regional
  allies: Hormuz disruption, selective direct missile or drone attacks, Houthi
  pressure near Bab el-Mandeb, and Iraqi-militia pressure from the north.
- The modal next-month sequence is proxy or maritime escalation, bounded U.S. or
  allied retaliation, and renewed mediation. A sustained regional war remains a
  material tail rather than the base case.
- Oil, refined products, LNG, fertilizer, helium, freight, food inputs, and long
  sovereign yields already carry conflict effects. A ceasefire headline would
  not instantly restore physical supply or insurance confidence.
- The November 2026 U.S. midterm election increases pressure for lower gasoline
  and diesel prices. It also creates an incentive for Washington to seek a
  visible coercive success before accepting a temporary pause.
- Ticker `LNG` is Cheniere Energy, a U.S. exporter from Louisiana and Texas. It
  does not need Hormuz to be open. A prolonged Hormuz closure removes competing
  Qatari supply and increases the strategic value of U.S. LNG.
- The original stock screen nevertheless overstated Cheniere's immediate
  commodity-price convexity. Approximately 90% of anticipated Cheniere
  production is contracted through the mid-2030s, largely with fixed-fee or
  take-or-pay-style economics. Cheniere remains a medium-term long candidate,
  but it is no longer ranked as the highest-conviction one-week trade.

## Factual baseline as of August 19

### Conflict and diplomacy

- U.S. and Israeli attacks began on February 28, 2026.
- The conflict had lasted nearly six months by August 19.
- The June 17 memorandum created a 60-day negotiation period. It expired without
  a final agreement.
- Washington and Tehran disagreed about whether the memorandum granted Iran a
  role administering Hormuz.
- Trump said no talks were taking place or scheduled on August 18. Other U.S.
  officials described wider back channels, including an IRGC channel.
- Iran said it was moving from a defensive posture to a "fully offensive"
  posture and threatened a timely attack to break the U.S. naval blockade if its
  deadline was not met.
- Commercial Hormuz traffic remained far below normal despite U.S. statements
  that the route was open.

### Revealed U.S. behavior pattern

1. Negotiate while applying sanctions or military pressure.
2. Set short deadlines and use maximalist public demands.
3. Escalate when talks or deterrence appear to fail.
4. Use ceasefire or deal language before physical flows normalize.
5. Use strategic reserves, waivers, insurance, and diplomatic pauses when fuel
   and inflation costs become politically damaging.
6. Seek an outcome that can be described domestically as a victory.

This is a behavioral summary of observed actions. It is not a claim that every
future U.S. decision will follow the same sequence.

### Revealed Iranian behavior pattern

1. Put regime survival above output, trade, or household welfare.
2. Negotiate while retaining asymmetric coercive tools.
3. Use Hormuz access as bargaining leverage over sanctions, assets, blockade
   relief, compensation, and military operations.
4. Escalate through geographically distributed partners and selective direct
   attacks rather than rely only on large direct exchanges with Israel or the
   United States.
5. Preserve ambiguity where useful, while sometimes accepting enough
   attribution to demonstrate reach.
6. Repress domestic unrest while using external conflict to support a national
   defense narrative.

The IRGC's wartime role makes elected-government conciliatory statements less
reliable as indicators of final policy.

### Why the allied-target pathway matters

The current network gives Iran three partially redundant pressure axes:

- East: Hormuz, Gulf shipping, and Gulf energy infrastructure.
- North: Iraqi territory and Iran-aligned armed groups near Saudi Arabia,
  Kuwait, Jordan, and U.S. facilities.
- South and west: Houthi attacks on Saudi territory, Red Sea infrastructure,
  tankers, and Bab el-Mandeb.

This can impair both Hormuz exports and the Saudi Red Sea bypass. It also forces
regional air defenses to cover many economic and military targets.

The evidence for coordination is meaningful but not conclusive. The strongest
claims of IRGC supervision came from Saudi and regional intelligence sources.
Some Houthi attack claims lacked independent Saudi confirmation. Iran disputed
UAE attribution of the August 18 missiles.

## How the scenario estimates were formed

### Scenario states

The estimates used six mutually exclusive end states at each horizon:

1. Hostile standoff: blockades, threats, low commercial flow, and no major new
   strike cycle.
2. Bounded escalation: direct or proxy attacks followed by limited retaliation,
   without sustained region-wide operations.
3. Major or wider war: sustained multi-front strikes, repeated attacks on major
   energy infrastructure, or substantial expansion of U.S. operations.
4. Temporary negotiated pause: an armistice, sequencing arrangement, or partial
   Hormuz reopening without resolution of the nuclear and regional disputes.
5. Durable settlement: an implemented agreement covering enforcement,
   sanctions, nuclear material, enrichment, and maritime passage.
6. Iranian regime crisis: leadership fracture, mass unrest, or loss of effective
   central control large enough to dominate the conflict path.

### Inputs used

The estimates were judgmental, not generated by a trained forecasting model.
The following inputs were considered explicitly:

- Current military posture and recent attacks.
- Whether official threats were paired with visible movement or prior execution.
- The success and durability of the April and June pauses.
- The size of the negotiating gap and sequencing conflict.
- IRGC influence and Iran's internal economic and unrest pressure.
- U.S. gasoline, diesel, inflation, fiscal, and midterm-election pressure.
- Israeli objectives, which are broader than immediate U.S. Hormuz objectives.
- Gulf-state incentives to mediate while preparing military responses.
- Shipping, insurance, inventory, refinery, LNG, and fertilizer evidence.
- The possibility that public statements are bargaining tactics rather than
  reliable operational guidance.

### Probability calibration

The probability process was:

1. Start with the currently observed state, not a pre-war prior.
2. Assign the largest near-term weight to continuation because state persistence
   is common and major decisions require triggers.
3. Raise bounded escalation when threats are paired with attacks, mobilization,
   or expiring deadlines.
4. Raise major-war risk only when the likely response chain crosses a clear
   threshold, such as U.S. fatalities or severe energy-infrastructure damage.
5. Raise temporary-pause probability as economic pain and the U.S. election
   window approach.
6. Keep durable settlement low while enrichment, inspection, sanctions,
   compensation, and Hormuz administration remain unresolved.
7. Increase regime-crisis probability gradually rather than treating inflation
   alone as evidence of imminent collapse.

The resulting percentages are analyst estimates with uncertainty of at least
5-10 percentage points. They are not market-implied probabilities.

### Move-range calibration

Stock move ranges were not produced by an optimizer or options-implied
calculation. They were bounded using:

- The scenario probability at the relevant horizon.
- Directness of the company's exposure to the shock.
- Whether the shock changes revenue, margin, volume, utilization, backlog, or
  only sentiment.
- Current price relative to the 52-week range.
- Recent one-year and year-to-date performance as a crowding check.
- Balance sheet, fixed versus variable pricing, and customer pass-through.
- The size of observed 2026 commodity and sector reactions.
- Whether earnings or contract revisions would occur inside the horizon.

The ranking was structured and ordinal. No claim is made that a precise formula
was executed. Conceptually, the score increased with directional probability,
conditional move size, causal exposure, and balance-sheet quality, and decreased
with crowding, valuation, pass-through, and thesis expiry.

## Cheniere Energy: why ticker LNG can benefit from closed Hormuz

### Direct answer

Ticker `LNG` does not depend on Hormuz being open. It is Cheniere Energy, not a
Qatari LNG cargo index.

Cheniere operates LNG export facilities at Sabine Pass, Louisiana, and Corpus
Christi, Texas. Its cargoes leave the U.S. Gulf Coast. They do not need to enter
or exit the Persian Gulf and therefore do not transit Hormuz.

A closed Hormuz is directionally positive for Cheniere because Qatar normally
provides about one-fifth of global LNG trade and almost all Qatari seaborne LNG
must leave the Persian Gulf. When that supply is unavailable, Europe and Asia
compete more aggressively for U.S. and other non-Hormuz cargoes.

### Why the benefit is real

- Cheniere had about 55 mtpa of operating liquefaction capacity at the second
  quarter 2026 report, with more than 6 mtpa under construction or commissioning.
- It exported 184 cargoes and 672 TBtu in the second quarter, up 19% and 22%,
  respectively, from the comparable period.
- It raised 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance from $7.25-$7.75 billion to
  $7.90-$8.40 billion.
- It raised Cheniere distributable cash flow guidance from $4.75-$5.25 billion
  to $5.30-$5.80 billion.
- It tightened its 2026 production forecast upward to 53-54 million tonnes.
- Corpus Christi Stage 3 trains added volume, with Train 7 expected to begin LNG
  production and reach substantial completion in the second half of 2026.
- Stronger global scarcity can increase the value of uncontracted cargoes,
  Cheniere Marketing volumes, short-term sales, destination flexibility, and
  future long-term contracting or expansion decisions.

### Why the benefit is less convex than the first ranking implied

Cheniere's 2025 Form 10-K says approximately 90% of anticipated production from
its liquefaction projects was contracted through the mid-2030s, with about 15
years of weighted-average remaining contract life.

The standard SPA structure contains:

- A fixed liquefaction fee that the customer generally pays even if it cancels
  or suspends a cargo.
- A variable fee generally equal to 115% of Henry Hub, intended to cover gas
  feedstock, transport, and liquefaction fuel.
- Contract design that limits Cheniere's net exposure to increases in U.S.
  natural-gas prices.

IPM agreements also generate take-or-pay-style fixed liquefaction economics.
Only production not committed under long-term contracts is available for spot or
short-term marketing, although early production before some long-term contracts
commence can temporarily enlarge that pool.

The 2025 revenue split illustrates the distinction:

- $14.804 billion of LNG revenue came from long-term agreements.
- $3.794 billion came from project LNG sold by the integrated marketing function
  under short-term agreements.
- 2,095 TBtu was delivered under long-term agreements versus 321 TBtu from
  project LNG under short-term agreements.

Therefore, a global LNG price spike does not multiply all Cheniere cash flow.
It supports a smaller marketing book, utilization, contract value, and growth
optionality around a much larger fixed-fee base.

### What happens if Hormuz stays closed

Likely positives:

- Qatari replacement demand persists.
- Europe and Asia bid harder for destination-flexible U.S. LNG.
- Marketing and early uncontracted train volumes can earn stronger margins.
- Buyers have more incentive to sign long-term U.S. supply contracts.
- Expansion capacity becomes strategically more valuable.

Limits and risks:

- Approximately 90% contract coverage limits spot upside.
- Cheniere must source U.S. feedgas and power. Higher domestic gas prices can
  still matter outside the protected contract structure.
- DAP cargoes require vessels. Gulf of Aden conflict, Panama congestion, war-risk
  insurance, or vessel shortages can raise delivery costs or interrupt routes.
- Cheniere had $22.632 billion of long-term debt at June 30, 2026.
- U.S. Gulf hurricanes, pipeline outages, construction delay, customer default,
  regulation, and export authorization remain company-specific risks.
- The stock already traded at $273.50 against a 52-week high of $300.89 in the
  reviewed snapshot, so some scarcity and growth value was already priced.

### What happens if Hormuz reopens

- Global LNG scarcity and spot spreads should fall.
- Cheniere Marketing and uncontracted-volume upside should compress.
- The equity could sell off on the headline, especially if it had accumulated a
  geopolitical premium.
- Fixed fees, take-or-pay-style economics, and Henry Hub pass-through should
  preserve much of the contract-backed cash-flow floor.
- A reopening therefore hurts the upside option more than the core contracted
  business.

## Invalidation and update rules

### Conflict indicators that raise escalation risk

- U.S. fatalities at a Gulf base, vessel, or diplomatic site.
- Verified coordinated Houthi and Iraqi-militia launches.
- Major damage to Abqaiq, Khurais, Ras Laffan, Fujairah, Yanbu, Jazan, or another
  systemically important energy asset.
- U.S. or Israeli attacks on Kharg Island, Iranian power infrastructure, or
  Iranian coastal territory.
- Another material decline in Hormuz or Bab el-Mandeb crossings.
- Withdrawal of mediators or closure of the IRGC back channel.

### Indicators that raise pause probability

- A written sequencing agreement rather than another "deal soon" statement.
- Verified blockade relief paired with verified Iranian passage measures.
- Several consecutive days of rising commercial crossings and loadings.
- Restored war-risk insurance without prohibitive exclusions.
- IAEA access to bombed sites and surviving enriched-uranium stocks.
- OFAC waivers that are implemented rather than merely announced.

### Cheniere-specific confirmation

- Corpus Christi Train 7 reaches substantial completion on schedule.
- Exported cargoes and TBtu continue rising.
- Adjusted EBITDA and distributable cash flow track the raised guidance.
- Cheniere Marketing volume and margin respond positively to the global shortage.
- New long-term SPAs support expansion decisions at acceptable returns.
- Buybacks, dividends, and debt reduction continue without impairing project
  funding.

### Cheniere-specific invalidation

- A sustained Hormuz reopening restores Qatari exports faster than expected.
- European storage rises enough to remove the winter scarcity bid.
- Global LNG spreads compress while Henry Hub or U.S. basis costs rise.
- U.S. Gulf weather or pipeline problems reduce feedgas or liquefaction uptime.
- Train 7 or later expansions slip materially or exceed expected cost.
- Customer performance, shipping availability, regulatory approval, or export
  authorization deteriorates.

## Limits and no-evidence findings

- No claim is made that Iran ordered every Houthi or Iraqi-militia attack. The
  strongest coordination evidence remains source-attributed intelligence and
  regional assessments.
- No claim is made that the UAE's attribution of every missile or vessel attack
  is independently verified. Iran disputed key allegations.
- The conflict probabilities are not statistical-model output and are not
  market-implied.
- The stock move ranges are not backtested confidence intervals, option-implied
  distributions, or analyst price targets.
- Borrow costs and option premiums were not collected. Short rankings therefore
  describe directional equity vulnerability, not expected net trade profit.
- Intraday prices and 52-week ranges are delayed observations and can change
  before the note is read.
- Company-specific earnings, regulation, execution, valuation, weather, and
  capital-allocation events can dominate the geopolitical thesis.
- No position sizing, entry price, stop, tax treatment, liquidity need, or user
  risk tolerance was modeled.
- A temporary ceasefire can produce a sharp commodity reversal even when the
  long-run conflict remains unresolved.
